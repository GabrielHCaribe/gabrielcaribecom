In September, 2026, I few on the NRC's Falcon 20 to shoot lasers and float around in 0g.

Context

I was the Electrical Lead for team Cristar, a Universtiy of Toronto team who participated in the Canadain Reduced Gravity Experiment Challenge (CAN-RGX 9) organized by SEDS Canada. Every year, out of dozens of student teams who submit applications, 4 teams are selected to bring a science experiment onboard the National Research Council's Falcon 20 aircraft to make measuremnts in 0g and 2g. This year, our team won the Overall Excellence Award.

Our goal was to create protien crystals in 0g and 2g using laser-induced crystalization (shooting a Class 4 laser at a solution to cause crystals to grow quickly) to see if reduced gravity can improve crystal structure on short-duration flights.

What we built

Our experiment mainly consisted of the following components:

  • Glucoloop Ivy Mid-Infrared Nanosecond Laser
  • Zaber linear translation stage and the solution to be crystallized
  • Rapberry Pi 5
  • Humidity, temperature, and acceleration sensors
  • Pelican Case
  • Graphical User Interface

The pelican case was the housing for the experiment. Inside, there were 2 levels. The bottom level had the laser which shot photons at a mirror that directed the beam downwards into a well-plate which housed the sample. The well-plate was mounted on a Zaber linear translation stage which moved to place one well at a time under the focus of the laser.

The top level held the sensors, dessicant to keep the humidity down, a camera, and the Raspberry Pi which was connected to a laptop outside of the case, through which we controlled the experiment.

As the electrical lead, I was in charge of initial component selection (stage, camera, sensors, etc.). I also designed the first iteration of the mirror mount and the camera mount in SOLIDWORKS.

The Flight

The Falcon 20 does a parabolic flight - it goes up on a steep angle, then down on a steep angle. This means that it experiences a 20s period of hypergravity (~2g), followed by 20s of weightlessness (~0g) when it is levening off and starting to dip, followed by hypergravity again when it is raising out of the dip.

2 operators are on board running the experiemnt. Our team has one member controlling the GUI on the laptop - they were the one actually clicking to fire the laser, and the other operator tracked the stage of the flight (how much gravity they were under) to tell the first operator when to fire the laser. On my flight, I was the second operator, and Syeda Mahdia (Engineering Physics at U of T, 2T7) was the first operator.

Experiencing microgravity was a childhood dream come true. I want to give a huge thank you to the rest of my team: Alexander Wainwright (Team Lead, Physics PhD student), Syeda Mahdia (Science Lead, Engineering Physics), Elias Barsa (Mechanical Lead, Engineering Physics), Natalie Duric (Outreach, Physics and Astronomy), Ian Mo (Outreach, Physics and Astronomy), Daniel Yu (Electrical, Computer Engineering), Lauren Altomare (Science, Biomedical Engineering), and Khaled (Support, Physics PhD student).
