Overview

Blue Ocean Strategy by W. Chan Kim and Renée Maes gives actionable frameworks for building a company strategy that exits the "red ocean" of bloody competition, and enters a "blue ocean" where you dominate the market by creating your own market.

How do you do this?

  • Assess the current state of the industry and your place in it
  • Formulate and Execute a Blue Ocean Strategy

Assessing the Industry with a Strategy Canvas

A strategy canvas is an actionable framework used to diagnose the playing field and shows you where competition is investing their time and money and what they're competing on

To make one, find the factors your industry competes on and put them on the x-axis, and then rank how heavily companies sinvet in thoses areas relative to their invetments in other facotrs and other companys. An example strategy canvas from the book is shown below, created for the U.S wine industry:

It is important to not try to beat competition be offering a little more for a little less. This will lead to a race to the bottom where you squeeze your margins to nothing instead of creating new value - you want to make competition irrelevent, not just slightly outperform them.

To shift strategy canvas, change strategy focus from competitors to alternatives, and from customers to noncustomers of the industry.

How do you do this though?

4 Actions Framework

  • Reduce: Which factors should be reduced well below industry standards
  • Create: Which factors should be created that the industry has never offered
  • Raise: Which factors should be raised well above industry standards
  • Eliminate: Which of the factors the industry takes for granted should be eliminated

Answering all of these leads to a new value curve. Folloign the previous example, the book explains how the wine company [yellow tail] did this to quickly grow to being one of the most bought wines in the U.S.

They shifted their focus from hardcore wine drinkers away to noncustomers being general alcohol (beer, cocktail, etc.) consumers. They realized that this new demographic did not care about the pretegious parts of wine (fancy terminology, aging quality, prestige, etc.) so they eliminated or strongly reduced these factors. While they're competitors were expending lots of resources trying to be the more presigious, they invested in facotrs that others weren't even considering like Fun and Adventure. 3 Characteristics of a Good Strategy

  • Focus
  • Divergence
  • Compelling Tagline that Speaks to the Market

If your Strategy Canvas looks like you're trying to be the top at everything, you are not focused enough. You should be able to clearly see the areas where you are investing heavily conpared to competition, and the areas you are choosing to be far below competition. Your canvas should thus look digergent from competitors' canvases.

Your strategy should be simple to explain in a short and compelling tagline - if you can't do this, you are probably not focused enough. For example, [yellow tail]'s tagline could be "A fun adn simple win to be enjoyed every day."

The labels on your strategy canvas should be in terms buyers understand and value

How to Formulate a Blue Ocean Strategy

After having assessed the market with a strategy canvas, there are 4 principles to formulating your strategy:

  1. Reconstruct Market Boundaries
  2. Focus on the Big Picture, Not the Numbers
  3. Reach Beyond Existing Demand
  4. Get the strategy sequence right

Reconstructing Market Boundaries

Companies tend to want to compete on similar factors as the rest of their industry and focus on being the best within those bounds. The authors found 6 ways in which companies tend to stay similar to their competitors. It is important to know these paths and plan how you can diverge in them to create value your competitors don't offer.

6 Paths Framework

Path 1: Look across alternative industries

Alternaties industries are different forms of a solution to the same problem. For example, restauraunts and movie theatres are alternatives to providing people with entertainment - each offers value to different people.

Ask Yourself:

  • What are the alternatives to my industry
  • What do customers trade across them?

You want to find the best parts and reduce or eliminate everything else.

For example, NetJets looked at corperate customers in the airline travel industry and saw that they has 2 options: business class on a public airline or buying a company jet. They took the best parts of each alternative and offered private jet partial ownership, providing speed and comfort without all the maintnance and immediate costs.

Path 2: Look across strategic groups within your industry

Strategic groups are a group of companied in an industry that have similar strategies, generally ranked in hierarchical order baesd on price and performance.

You need to understand why customers trade up or down strategic groups to create a blue ocean.

An example of this is how Toyota'd Lexus created a blue ocean by offering high quality cars comparable to BMW and Mercedes at prices similar to lower-end Cadillac and Lincon.

Ask Yourself:

  • What are strategic groups in my industry?
  • Why do customers trade up and why do they trade down?

Path 3: Look across the chain of buyers

Often, the people who buy a product are different than the people who use it.

For example, the pharmasutical indutry used to heavily focus on selling to doctors. The company Novo Nordisk shifted this in their strategy by creating an insulin injection pen that was much easier and nicer for users. They created a blue ocean by selling to the users rather than the doctors.

Another important concept relevant to this idea but not from the book is to influence the influencer:

Example, a chocolate company reached out to Kim Kardashian's chef, got him to like their chocolate, which then led to him recommending it to Kim. Since she trusts him (he is an influencer to her), she then went around eating it, influencing others to buy it too.

Ask Yourself:

  • What is the chain of buyers in my industry?
  • Which buyer group does the industry typically focus on?
  • How could designing for a different buyer change the product to be more appealing?

Path 4: Look Across Complimentary Products and Service Offerings

Products and services do not exist on their own. Other factors affect their value.

For example, needing to get a babysitter affects the value that movie theaters offer, making them less appealing to parents with young children.

The bus manufacturer NABI took advantage of this path. They saw that bus companies selling to municipalities were focused on selling the cheapest bus. They overlooked the maintnance costs and environmental impact of their busses though. Seeing this, NABI made their busses of fiberglass, increasing the bus' initial price but making its lifetime cost lower and being the more environemtnally friendly option.

Ask Yourself:

  • What is the context my product is used?
  • What happens before, during, and after?
  • Find pain points and eliminate them.

Path 5: Look Across Functional or Emotional Appeal to Buyers

Indutries typically appeal to customers either on functionality or emotion.

Differing your appeal from that of your competition can lead to a blue ocean strategy.

For example, Swatch transitioned to an emotional appeal having their watches be a fashion statement. Their competition in the budget watch space mainly focued on the functionality of their watched, setting Swatch apart from them.

Ask Yourself:

  • Is my industry compete on functional or emotional appeal?
  • What elements can I switch to be the opposite?

Path 6: Look Across Time

Look for decisive, irreversible trends that have a clear trajectory.

For example, Apple capitalized on the illegal music downloading trend by offering iTunes. They made songs ccheaper to buy through offering individual songs to be purchased for 99 cents rather than needing to buy entire albums, made the experience easier and fasert, and offered much better sound quality than illegal downloads.

After spotting a trend, look into the future and ask what would the market look like if the trend were taken to its logical conclusion

Ask Yourself:

  • Which trends have a high probability of impacting my industry, are irreversible, and are evolving in a clear trajectory?
  • How will these trends impact my industry?
  • How can I use this to open up unprecedented customer utility?

2. Focusing on the Big Picture, Not the Numbers

Companies often make their strategy plans too complicated with poor information from different parts of their company that don't communcate well. Drawing and using a strategy canvas is much more beneficial. This section briefly discusses the process for drawing and discussing strategy canvas.

  1. Visual Awakening:
    • It is easy to fall into the trap of brainstorming changes in your strategy before fully understanding the playing field
    • Start by drawing the strategy canvas for your business and competitors "as is"
  2. Visual Exploration
    • Go into the field (actually talk to customers and noncustomers) to explore the 6 paths to creating blue oceans
    • Observe advantages of alternative products and services
    • Decide what to eliminate, create, reduce, or change
  3. Visual Strategy Fair
    • Draw your to-be strategy canvas
    • Get feedback on it from customers, competitors' customers, and non-customers
    • Incorperate the feedback
  4. Visual Communication
    • Distribute before and after strategy profiles on one page for comparison
    • Only support projects that close the gap to your new strategy

3. Reach Beyond Existing Demand

Instead of concentrating on cutomers, you need to look to noncustomers, and instead of focuing on customer differences, you mut find the commonalitie in what buyers value.

Explain 1st, 2nd, and 3rd tier non-customers and how to use them

4. Get the Strategic Sequence Right

Buyer utility -> Price -> cost -> adoptation
